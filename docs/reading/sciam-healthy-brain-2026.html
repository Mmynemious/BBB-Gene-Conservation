Scientific American Special Editions, “The Healthy Brain”. Volume 35, Issue 3, September 2026. Cover marked Summer/Fall 2026, display until 8 December 2026. https://www.scientificamerican.com/issue/special-editions/2026/09-01/

Why I read it

To get a sense of what the field has been up to, outside the narrow question this project asks. Reading only the papers that are directly about conservation makes it easy to lose track of what the wider brain research world thinks is important.

Articles

Brainwashing, by Lydia Denworth

Published online as “How your brain’s nightly cleanse keeps it healthy”, 19 August 2025.

Topic. Sleep and the clearance of waste from the brain by cerebrospinal fluid.

What stuck with me. CSF travels through the brain and essentially acts like a sewage system, taking things out of the brain that can be taken out, so it is cleaning waste. The fluid can pass down along the spinal column, or exit the nervous system via the lymphatic system, or through valve-like structures called arachnoid granulations that give it access to the blood circulation.

What they are showing in this article is that sleep can be a very important step in clearing out the brain.

They also say this CSF cleaning system can depend on sound. They looked at different age classes, specifically older people, and tried to understand how the frequencies they record from the CSF behave in correlation with sound frequencies played to people while they are sleeping. Apparently there seems to be a correlation, where the higher the frequency the more the CSF is cleaning the brain. I found that interesting to see, though that is just my understanding of it.

Lead to follow up. The primary work behind the sound and frequency claim, since that is the part I would want to see properly before believing it.

Why your brain needs exercise, by David A. Raichlen and Gene E. Alexander

What stuck with me. Not really part of the blood brain barrier discussion, so it is here for completeness rather than because it matters to the project.

It did remind me of A Brief History of Intelligence by Max Bennett, where the argument is that bipedalism enabled a larger prefrontal cortex and a larger hippocampus, because of the new tasks we were then able to do, such as navigating our environment with goals and intentions.

Ozempic quiets the brain’s food noise, by Lauren J. Young

What stuck with me. Interesting to read, but it did not matter a lot for this project.

A wealth of new treatments, by Esther Landhuis

Topic. The current landscape of drug development for the brain. This was the most useful article in the issue.

Note: this piece does not appear in the online contents listing for the issue, which may be incomplete. Worth confirming the exact title and author from the printed copy.

What stuck with me. It shows a graph of drugs currently in development and which clinical phase they are in, organised by treatment target. The target classes named are:

  • neurotransmitter receptors
  • amyloid
  • inflammation and immune processes
  • tau
  • synaptic plasticity and neuroprotection
  • metabolism
  • vasculature
  • growth factors and hormones
  • epigenetic regulators
  • neurogenesis
  • oxidative stress
  • proteostasis
  • APOE

Vasculature is the class most closely related to what this project is doing. Under it they discuss, very briefly, two targets that can be inhibited by the vasculature itself, and they look at less leaky sections of the blood brain barrier.

The idea I took from this. It might be interesting to look at papers that study the leaky sections of the blood brain barrier, rather than only the tight ones. That could matter for understanding how drugs can actually be administered.

Barrier against brain disease, by Daniela Kaufer and Alon Friedman

Listed online as “Damage to a protective shield around the brain may lead to Alzheimer’s”, originally published May 2021.

Topic. What happens to the brain once the blood brain barrier starts to leak, and whether the damage that follows can be reversed. This was a very interesting paper to read.

Where it started. These two researchers came across Gulf War syndrome, where they found that pyridostigmine was reaching the brain when it was not supposed to. Soldiers were reporting long term tiredness and similar symptoms, and they believed the penetration of the blood brain barrier by pyridostigmine was the primary factor.

What they were speculating at the time is that stress would make the blood brain barrier leaky, allowing proteins that are normally not found past it to appear.

Their first experiments: they let mice swim in water to put them under a lot of stress, then injected blue dye into their veins. They found that these brains had a strong blue tint. So one of the first assumptions was that putting animals, or even people, under a lot of stress would cause the blood brain barrier to leak. They published these results in Nature Medicine.

The link to brain disease. Barrier damage in patients with some brain disorders does seem to point to Alzheimer’s patients, who seem to have a leaky blood brain barrier.

What they wanted to see was the brain function directly after the blood brain barrier started to malfunction, meaning once it gets leaky. They gave rats a chemical that poked holes in their barrier, then dissected their brains at various later times.

The storm. What they found was what they call a social media storm, as an analogy. There was a trigger in the blood brain barrier which suddenly allowed epilepsy like signals to appear, very suddenly. This storm happened only after they mimicked a blood brain barrier leak. They repeated it several times and it worked.

Then they injected blood into the rat’s brain, and came to the conclusion that specifically albumin is responsible for this reaction. They were not thrilled with this catch, because albumin is in the blood and the blood fulfils a lot of different tasks in our body. So before running the experiments again, they tried to understand what albumin does in the blood brain barrier context.

The mechanism, as I understand it. When albumin contacts an astrocyte it binds on TGF beta. TGF beta is an indicator for inflammation, or can cause inflammation, and when you have inflammation cytokines are released. On the other hand, TGF beta also activates the astrocyte and the microglia.

Albumin provides a positive feedback loop and an overproduction of TGF beta. So there is an over-activation of astrocytes and microglia, which in turn activates more TGF beta, which in turn causes more inflammation and cytokines. This is essentially the stress they had recorded in mice previously, which also made the blood brain barrier leaky.

They also say that astrocytes become senescent, that is, chemically active even after their role in the feedback loop is done.

Age. They compared albumin concentrations in the brain depending on the age of mice, and found that albumin in younger mice was not apparent, but started to show up when they were in middle age. They checked this for humans and it seems to be the same case: heightened albumin levels accompanied greater amounts of TGF beta in astrocytes, and also in people who had neurological diseases.

Correlation or causation. This is where it gets interesting. They infused albumin into the intact healthy brains of young mice for one week, and these brains started to work and function like older brains, with abnormal neuronal function.

To make sure the experiment was not flawed by TGF beta, they cut out the TGF beta cascade that came after the leak, so that you are really seeing a causation result. It turned out that the mice with the receptors and with albumin in their brains had a lot of trouble getting out of the water maze, while the young mice without the TGF beta cascade behaved in a very normal way and found their way out.

The drug. You cannot knock out TGF beta in humans, that is too risky. Instead they were contacted by a medical startup drug company, and were given a drug called IPW, which is an inhibitor for the activity of TGF beta.

They gave the mice the IPW drug, and it turned out that these mice, even though they had had abnormal functions for a very long time, were able to regain their brain functions to the state they were in before albumin was injected. So it seems like the brain is able to rebound from some types of abnormal activity.

What this changed for me. When I think about diseases like Alzheimer’s, I think the damage done to the brain is permanent, and that when a brain deteriorates it is not possible to get it back to a state where it functions normally, even if you had a magical drug that cancelled out the negative effects.

It turns out it is possible. The brain has some hidden feedback loop, some kind of mechanism that allows it to regain normal function, to such an extent that even the researchers were quite surprised, because the mice that were injected with albumin and then got the IPW drug were able to swim out of the maze.

Worth confirming from the printed copy. The exact drug designation and the name of the company that supplied it. IPW is most likely IPW-5371, a TGF beta receptor inhibitor.

What the field seems to be up to

The drug development graph is the thing I keep coming back to. The sheer number of target classes says that nobody has settled on a mechanism, and that the vasculature is only one route among a dozen being tried at once. That is worth knowing when this project treats BBB genes as though they are the obvious place to look.

Leads worth turning into entries

Primary work to find and read properly, then add to the literature review.

On the leaky barrier. This is the strongest lead and the one that connects directly to the project. If the barrier is not uniformly tight, then a gene list built from average enrichment across a whole microvessel fraction is describing an average of two different things. That bears on step 1 and on the brain region field in how each paper is read.

The primary work behind Kaufer and Friedman. Parked for now. The article summarises roughly twenty five years of their own research and does so well enough for orientation, which is all this section is for. Worth returning to only if the reversibility result, or barrier breakdown in ageing, becomes something the project actually uses. In that case, to find and verify:

  • The original stress and pyridostigmine work in Nature Medicine, around 1996, which is the blue dye experiment described in the article.
  • The albumin and TGF beta work on epileptogenesis, which is where the epilepsy like signals and the albumin mechanism come from.
  • The ageing study showing albumin accumulation with age, hyperactivation of TGF beta signalling, and reversal with the TGF beta receptor inhibitor. This is the one that matches the water maze and IPW part of the article most closely, and the one most worth reading first, since it carries the reversibility result.

Note the distinction. Kaufer and Friedman are about a barrier that fails, in disease and with age. The question this project ran into is different: whether the healthy barrier is uniformly tight along the vessel tree, or whether tight and leaky segments are being averaged together in a bulk microvessel fraction. That is regional and segmental heterogeneity, not pathology, and it is a separate literature.

Sweeney et al. 2018, the review of blood brain barrier physiology and disease, is already downloaded and covers both, so it is the sensible single starting point if either thread is picked up.

On sleep and CSF clearance. The glymphatic literature is the place to start, and the sound and frequency angle should trace back to work on CSF oscillations coupled to slow wave activity during sleep. Worth checking whether the magazine’s framing survives contact with the primary paper, since “higher frequency means more cleaning” is the kind of claim that gets tidied up in the retelling.

On drainage routes. Arachnoid granulations and the meningeal lymphatics are both well studied. Relevant here only as a reminder that the barrier is one part of a larger system of exchange between brain, blood and lymph, which this project’s framing leaves out entirely.
