Barrier against brain disease, by Daniela Kaufer and Alon Friedman

Scientific American Special Editions, The Healthy Brain, Fall 2026

Author

Yara Ismail

Published

September 23, 2026

Back to the issue notes

Listed online as “Damage to a protective shield around the brain may lead to Alzheimer’s”, originally published May 2021.

Topic. What happens to the brain once the blood brain barrier starts to leak, and whether the damage that follows can be reversed. This was a very interesting paper to read.

Where it started. These two researchers came across Gulf War syndrome, where they found that pyridostigmine was reaching the brain when it was not supposed to. Soldiers were reporting long term tiredness and similar symptoms, and they believed the penetration of the blood brain barrier by pyridostigmine was the primary factor.

What they were speculating at the time is that stress would make the blood brain barrier leaky, allowing proteins that are normally not found past it to appear.

Their first experiments: they let mice swim in water to put them under a lot of stress, then injected blue dye into their veins. They found that these brains had a strong blue tint. So one of the first assumptions was that putting animals, or even people, under a lot of stress would cause the blood brain barrier to leak. They published these results in Nature Medicine.

The link to brain disease. Barrier damage in patients with some brain disorders does seem to point to Alzheimer’s patients, who seem to have a leaky blood brain barrier.

What they wanted to see was the brain function directly after the blood brain barrier started to malfunction, meaning once it gets leaky. They gave rats a chemical that poked holes in their barrier, then dissected their brains at various later times.

The storm. What they found was what they call a social media storm, as an analogy. There was a trigger in the blood brain barrier which suddenly allowed epilepsy like signals to appear, very suddenly. This storm happened only after they mimicked a blood brain barrier leak. They repeated it several times and it worked.

Then they injected blood into the rat’s brain, and came to the conclusion that specifically albumin is responsible for this reaction. They were not thrilled with this catch, because albumin is in the blood and the blood fulfils a lot of different tasks in our body. So before running the experiments again, they tried to understand what albumin does in the blood brain barrier context.

The mechanism, as I understand it. When albumin contacts an astrocyte it binds on TGF beta. TGF beta is an indicator for inflammation, or can cause inflammation, and when you have inflammation cytokines are released. On the other hand, TGF beta also activates the astrocyte and the microglia.

Albumin provides a positive feedback loop and an overproduction of TGF beta. So there is an over-activation of astrocytes and microglia, which in turn activates more TGF beta, which in turn causes more inflammation and cytokines. This is essentially the stress they had recorded in mice previously, which also made the blood brain barrier leaky.

They also say that astrocytes become senescent, that is, chemically active even after their role in the feedback loop is done.

Age. They compared albumin concentrations in the brain depending on the age of mice, and found that albumin in younger mice was not apparent, but started to show up when they were in middle age. They checked this for humans and it seems to be the same case: heightened albumin levels accompanied greater amounts of TGF beta in astrocytes, and also in people who had neurological diseases.

Correlation or causation. This is where it gets interesting. They infused albumin into the intact healthy brains of young mice for one week, and these brains started to work and function like older brains, with abnormal neuronal function.

To make sure the experiment was not flawed by TGF beta, they cut out the TGF beta cascade that came after the leak, so that you are really seeing a causation result. It turned out that the mice with the receptors and with albumin in their brains had a lot of trouble getting out of the water maze, while the young mice without the TGF beta cascade behaved in a very normal way and found their way out.

The drug. You cannot knock out TGF beta in humans, that is too risky. Instead they were contacted by a medical startup drug company, and were given a drug called IPW, which is an inhibitor for the activity of TGF beta.

They gave the mice the IPW drug, and it turned out that these mice, even though they had had abnormal functions for a very long time, were able to regain their brain functions to the state they were in before albumin was injected. So it seems like the brain is able to rebound from some types of abnormal activity.

What this changed for me. When I think about diseases like Alzheimer’s, I think the damage done to the brain is permanent, and that when a brain deteriorates it is not possible to get it back to a state where it functions normally, even if you had a magical drug that cancelled out the negative effects.

It turns out it is possible. The brain has some hidden feedback loop, some kind of mechanism that allows it to regain normal function, to such an extent that even the researchers were quite surprised, because the mice that were injected with albumin and then got the IPW drug were able to swim out of the maze.

Worth confirming from the printed copy. The exact drug designation and the name of the company that supplied it. IPW is most likely IPW-5371, a TGF beta receptor inhibitor.
