My reading

I looked at the paper called Positive Selection on Mammalian Immune Genes: Effects of Gene Function and Selective Constraint. What the researchers did, they wanted to look at the immune genes that are targets for positive selection. Positive selection is a process in which genes are favoured and reproduced if their function ensures a higher likelihood of survival.

They took rodents to analyse the mammalian immune genes. They looked at gene function, gene expression, protein-protein interaction, and also the selective constraint. They looked at pattern recognition receptors, PRRs, which were expected to have a higher positive selection rate than other genes. They also expected that for cytokines and for cell surface proteins. And they reasoned that the gene function might determine the positive selection.

I noted down that they worked a lot with selective constraint, pN divided by pS, and also dN divided by dS, which we also used when we looked at the CDS.

So the focus is essentially that they wanted to see what immune genes were key targets for positive selection, and whether the enrichment of signatures of positive selection in certain genes is a direct effect of gene function or an indirect effect of other factors.

They looked at the Cricetidae and Muridae families, and they found that there were around 1,100 genes that they could divide into six categories, which proved their initial assumptions. The genes that they used were screened against other sources to check whether the immune function was covered by at least two sources. They also needed this categorization to separate the host pathogen genes from the other ones, because the host pathogen genes have a co-evolution process which the other genes might not have, and this might also influence the positive selection.

They also had control genes. I am not quite sure why this approach works, in which they just took randomly 1,300 protein-coding mouse genes which are not associated with immune related processes. So I am thinking that when they take random genes, which should not express a high positive selection because they are not related to the immune system in any way, they can use this as a baseline for their genes that do show a higher positive selection. I think.

They tested for positive selection with dN/dS. They looked at the CDS for the dN/dS, but they noticed that the CDS rarely allows for a positive selection calculation, so they used BUSTED. I will say I skipped this because I did not think it was very important for me.

What they saw is that immune genes had signatures of positive selection, including Tlr1 and Ifih1, and there were also some in the cytokines. Then they compared the full protein CDS with Dunnett’s test, to check, I believe, for a correlation. They saw that there is a higher positive selection in cytokines and cell surface proteins, and the differences in pN/pS match the different categories they assigned the immune genes to based on their gene function.

In the discussion they note that PRRs, cytokines and cell surface proteins had similar, higher proportions of genes with signatures of positive selection than control genes. But for the PRR genes, since there are not that many in the genome that they could use in a bioinformatical way, they state that there is limited statistical power, so it cannot be completely generalized. It does give you a hint that there might be a higher positive selection. They also note the co-evolution of host and pathogen, which I also kind of skipped. And they discuss why cytokines and cell surface proteins are common targets of pathogen immune evasion, and how that raises the proportion of positive selection in those genes.

What I take from it

They worked a lot with pN/pS and dN/dS, which we also used when we looked at the CDS. And the random control genes, which are not related to the immune system in any way, work as a baseline for the genes that do show a higher positive selection.

Checks against the paper

  • Immune cells or immune genes? Genes. The title is Positive Selection on Mammalian Immune Genes.
  • Around 1,100 genes in six categories? 1,273 immune genes were collected, 1,112 fit one of the six categories, and 977 passed alignment filters. The six are PRRs, cytokines, other cell surface proteins, intracellular signalling, extracellular proteases, and effectors.
  • Covered by at least two sources? Yes, at least two of four: Amigo2, KEGG, PANTHER and Reactome.
  • Why do random control genes work? As you thought, they are the background rate. Controls are not free of positive selection, 8.9% of them showed it against 13.0% of immune genes. The question is whether immune genes sit above that.
  • The CDS rarely allows a positive selection calculation? BUSTED still runs on the full CDS. It asks whether some sites on some branches have dN/dS above 1, which an average over the whole gene rarely shows.
  • Dunnett’s test for a correlation? Not a correlation. It compares each category against the control group.
  • Higher selection in cytokines and cell surface proteins? Yes. But once pN/pS was accounted for, only cytokines stayed higher than controls. The authors read cytokines as a direct effect of function, and cell surface proteins as mostly an indirect effect of low constraint.
  • Expression and protein-protein interaction? Neither predicted positive selection once the category was taken into account.
  • Limited power for PRRs? Yes. 3 of 56 PRRs were under positive selection, so the difference from controls was not significant.

Open questions from this read

  • How does BUSTED work, and why is it preferred over CODEML here? Skipped on this read.
  • The host pathogen co-evolution part of the discussion. Skipped on this read.

How it relates to this project

  1. It uses the same measure. dN/dS on aligned coding sequence is what this project uses for conservation, so it is a worked example of that measure on a published gene set.

  2. It uses a control set the same way. Random genes outside the category of interest as a baseline is the comparison this project makes between BBB genes and its control genes.

  3. It names an established algorithm. BUSTED is a candidate for a positive selection test in the method-driven pipeline, next to the dN/dS ratio itself.

Reference detail from the paper

  • Proportion under positive selection (BUSTED model-average): 89 of 1,005 control genes (8.9%) against 127 of 977 immune genes (13.0%), P = 0.0030. At FDR q < 0.2: 20 (2%) against 37 (3.8%).
  • By category: cytokines and cell surface proteins higher than controls (Dunnett’s P = 0.014 and 0.023). Effectors and proteases each under 50 genes, with only one or two selected.
  • dN/dS: every immune category except intracellular signalling higher than controls; signalling lower (P = 0.0005).
  • pN/pS: mean of house mouse (Harr et al. 2016, German samples, N = 8) and bank vole (Lundberg et al. 2020, N = 31). PRRs, cytokines and cell surface proteins higher than controls (P ≤ 0.013); signalling lower (P = 0.0029).
  • Named genes under positive selection: Ifih1 and Tlr1 (PRRs), Ifnb1 and Il12b (cytokines), Ifngr2 and Il12rb1 (cytokine receptors), Cd80 (cell surface). Full list in supplementary table S2.
