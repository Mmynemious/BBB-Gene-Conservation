Literature Review

What is already known about BBB gene conservation, translational models, and comparative genomics of brain barriers

Why this review exists

The project measures conservation. It does not, on its own, establish what conservation means for translational validity — whether a gene being 84% identical between mouse and human predicts that a drug acting on it will behave the same way in both. That question belongs to the literature, and this review is where the answer, or the absence of one, gets recorded.

A second job: the audit found that three of the four Daneman source tables were the wrong tables and that a paper logged as the macaque dataset contains no macaque samples. Both were reading errors, not analysis errors. Re-reading the sources carefully, on the record, is the correction.

Theme 1 — Defining the blood–brain barrier transcriptome

How the field decided which genes “are” BBB genes, and how much that definition varies between studies.

Daneman et al. 2010

Daneman R, Zhou L, Agalliu D, Cahoy JD, Kaushal A, Barres BA (2010). The mouse blood–brain barrier transcriptome: a new resource for understanding the development and function of brain endothelial cells. PLOS ONE 5(10): e13741.

Bears on: step1 — this is the source of the master gene list. Status: re-read, error found and characterised.

The supplementary tables are not interchangeable, and the pipeline treated them as if they were. S3 is a pericyte-specific gene list — Pdgfrb, Kcnj8, Vtn, Zic1 — not core BBB genes. S4 and S5 are developmentally up- and down-regulated CNS vascular genes, not the brain-vs-liver and brain-vs-lung endothelial contrasts the pipeline assumed. S6 is the BBB-enriched table: Slc2a1 (GLUT1), Slco1a4, Slc7a5, Ocln, Abcb1a.

The tell was a 1-gene overlap with Munji, which was the correct alarm and the wrong diagnosis — S3 was not too restrictive, it was a different cell type. Using S6 gives 151 of 411 genes overlapping Munji, which is what two studies of the same tissue should look like.

Annotations: to add.

Munji et al. 2019

Munji RN, et al. (2019). Profiling the mouse brain endothelial transcriptome in health and disease. Nature Neuroscience 22(11): 1892–1902.

Bears on: step1. Status: read, parsed correctly.

518 BBB-enriched mouse genes from sheet BBB-enriched of S5. This is the one source table in step 1 that was read as intended.

Annotations: to add.

Theme 2 — Human brain vascular atlases

Single-cell work that makes it possible to ask whether a mouse-derived gene list holds in human tissue.

Wälchli et al. 2024

Wälchli T, et al. (2024). Single-cell atlas of the human brain vasculature. Nature 614: 945–954.

Bears on: step2, step4b. Status: read.

76,125 sorted human brain vascular cells resolving 12 endothelial subtypes. Processed correctly, then reduced by step4b to a single “expressed above zero” test that 88.5% of all genes pass. The subtype resolution — the most valuable thing about the dataset — was never used. Whether a gene is capillary-specific rather than merely present is the question this atlas can answer and the pipeline never asked.

Annotations: to add.

Yang et al. 2022

Yang AC, et al. (2022). A human brain vascular atlas reveals diverse mediators of Alzheimer’s risk. Nature 603: 885–892.

Bears on: step4b. Status: re-read needed — only one sheet was used.

The supplementary file has seven sheets: Arterial, Capillary, Venous, SMC, Pericyte, Fibroblast, Astrocyte. It was read without naming a sheet, so only the first loaded. The column labelled as human validation actually means “is an arterial marker”.

Annotations: to add.

Winkler et al. 2022

Winkler EA, et al. (2022). A single-cell atlas of the normal and malformed human brain vasculature. Science 375(6584): eabi7377.

Bears on: step4b. Status: read, parsed correctly.

Annotations: to add.

Theme 3 — Translational models and cross-species validity

The literature this project most needs and currently has least of: what is known about whether sequence conservation predicts pharmacological transferability between species.

Giger et al. 2010 — withdrawn from the dataset list

Giger T, et al. (2010). Evolution of neuronal and endothelial transcriptomes in primates.

Bears on: the macaque arm. Status: withdrawn — it is not what it was logged as.

Recorded as the macaque dataset. Its 13 samples are all human (taxid 9606); the macaque data in that line of work belongs to Khaitovich et al. under a separate accession that was never downloaded. It also compares neurons to endothelium rather than brain to peripheral endothelium, so it is not a BBB gene list for any species.

Consequence for the project: there is currently no macaque expression evidence. Real macaque DNA is used throughout steps 5a–5h, but every gene earned its place on the list from a mouse study. The macaque arm rests on mouse-defined biology, and that assumption has never been tested.

Candidate replacement — rhesus macaque brain atlas

Science Advances (2023). Single-nucleus atlas of the rhesus macaque brain, ~1M nuclei across 28 regions. Available via CellxGene.

Status: identified, not yet obtained. Adding it widens the project’s scope and needs Dr. Clelland’s sign-off.

Theme 4 — Methods for measuring selection

To add

Background on dN/dS estimation, saturation at long divergence times, and the case for phylogenetic methods (PAML codeml) over pairwise estimates — which is a named limitation of this project.

How this project fits in

Once the three breaking defects are fixed, this project contributes a three-species coding-sequence comparison of a defined BBB gene set against a tissue control, with the mouse–macaque leg included so the triangle closes. What it will not contribute, without new data, is any statement about macaque BBB expression, or about regulatory sequence — the analysis is coding-sequence only, and regulatory regions are where most cross-species expression differences are thought to live.
